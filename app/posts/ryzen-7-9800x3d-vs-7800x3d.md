![AMD Ryzen 7 9800X3D vs 7800X3D Benchmarks](/images/posts/ryzen-7-9800x3d-vs-7800x3d.jpg)

Most comparison reviews between the Ryzen 7 9800X3D and 7800X3D focus on average FPS charts. And in those charts, the 9800X3D wins by maybe 9%, sometimes 12%. At 1440p with a decent GPU pushing high utilization, those numbers compress even further. Easy to look at that and conclude AMD just squeezed out another incremental refresh.

But that conclusion falls apart once you open the thermal logs and start paying attention to frametime percentiles instead of averages.

## The Packaging Problem AMD Had to Fix

I want to talk about the silicon stacking first because it explains everything else.

On the 7800X3D, the 64MB 3D V-Cache SRAM die sits on top of the compute cores. Physically on top. TSMC bonds it there using copper through-silicon vias — tiny vertical metal pillars connecting the two dies electrically. For cache latency, this works great. For heat? It's a problem.

Silicon conducts heat, but not spectacularly well. Somewhere around 130 to 150 W/m·K at room temperature, and it gets worse as the die heats up. So you end up with a chunk of passive SRAM sitting between the transistors generating all the heat and the metal lid (IHS) that's supposed to conduct it out to your cooler. AMD's solution was to just… limit the chip. The 7800X3D tops out at 5.05 GHz boost, runs at restricted voltages, and multiplier overclocking is locked entirely.

Whether that conservative approach would have "cooked" the cache cells without it — honestly, I'm not sure anyone outside AMD's packaging team knows the exact failure margin. But the thermal overhead clearly wasn't there. They had to leave performance on the table.

The 9800X3D flips the stack. Cache die goes to the bottom, sitting on the organic substrate. Compute CCD goes on top, where it can press directly against the underside of the IHS through the solder thermal interface. AMD also fills the empty space next to the cache die with structural dummy silicon — without it, the cooler's mounting pressure wouldn't distribute evenly across the package and you'd risk cracking the die.

End result: heat from the cores takes a shorter, less resistive path to your cooling solution. AMD got enough thermal margin back to push boost clocks to 5.2 GHz and unlock multiplier overclocking.

## Test Setup and a Note About the Cooler Choice

One thing that bothered me about most 9800X3D launch coverage was the cooling. Reviewers were strapping on Noctua NH-D15s, Arctic Liquid Freezer 360s, custom loops. Those are useful for finding the chip's absolute ceiling, but they don't tell you much about what happens in a normal build where someone spends $30–40 on an air cooler and calls it a day.

So the cooler here is a Thermalright Phantom Spirit 120 SE. Thirty-five dollars. Single tower. Fan locked at 950 RPM, which is barely audible from a meter away.

Rest of the bench:
- ASUS ROG Strix B650E-F Gaming WiFi, BIOS 3024 (AGESA 1.2.0.2a)
- Honeywell PTM7950 phase-change pad as TIM
- 32GB DDR5-6000 CL30-38-38-96, running 1:1 with FCLK
- NVIDIA RTX 4090 Founders Edition, driver 566.03
- Windows 11 Pro 24H2, VBS and memory integrity disabled

I disabled VBS because it adds measurable overhead in some titles and I wanted the CPU comparison to be as clean as possible. If you run with it enabled (which is the default on most prebuilts), expect slightly lower numbers from both chips, but the relative gap should stay about the same.

Ambient room temperature was 21°C. I checked it with a standalone thermometer at the start and end of each test session — it drifted by half a degree at most.

## Thermal Results

Here's what HWInfo64 logged over 10-minute sustained runs:

| Condition | 7800X3D Power | 7800X3D Tdie | 9800X3D Power | 9800X3D Tdie |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| Idle | 19.4 W | 41.2°C | 18.8 W | 38.6°C |
| Cyberpunk 2077 | 67.2 W | 68.4°C | 76.8 W | 61.1°C |
| CS2 Competitive | 58.1 W | 63.8°C | 66.4 W | 57.2°C |
| Cinebench R23 all-core | 81.4 W | 78.9°C | 114.2 W | 81.4°C |

The gaming numbers are the interesting part. The 9800X3D pulls about 10W more than the 7800X3D during Cyberpunk because it's boosting higher and sustaining those frequencies — yet it's running over 7°C cooler on the same cooler at the same fan speed. That's not a minor delta. On a cheap single-tower air cooler, 61°C during a demanding open-world game is genuinely impressive for a chip pulling 77W.

The Cinebench row tells a different story. Under a full all-core AVX workload, the 9800X3D pulls 114W — nearly 33W more than the 7800X3D — but the temperature only climbs to 81.4°C. Not great, not terrible. You're not going to thermal throttle, but you're also not going to push a heavy overclock on this cooler during sustained rendering. Which is fine, because nobody buys a 3D V-Cache chip for Cinebench.

## Gaming Benchmarks

I tested four titles. All runs captured with CapFrameX, three passes of 180 seconds each, and I threw out the first run in every title to let shaders compile and caches warm up. Resolution was 1440p for everything except CS2, which I ran at 1080p because that's how competitive players actually use it.

The RTX 4090 is deliberate overkill here — I wanted the CPU to be the constraint as often as possible so the comparison is actually measuring processor differences rather than waiting on the GPU.

### Cyberpunk 2077: Phantom Liberty

Test loop was the Dogtown central market area. If you've benchmarked this section before, you know why — it's packed with NPC crowd simulation that hammers the main thread, and fast camera pans through the market stalls trigger bursts of geometry draw calls that expose frametime weaknesses.

| | Avg FPS | 1% Low | 0.1% Low |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| 7800X3D | 162.4 | 104.2 | 71.6 |
| 9800X3D | 178.1 | 123.8 | 89.4 |
| Delta | +9.6% | +18.8% | +24.8% |

Average FPS moved less than 10%. But look at the 1% low column — from 104 to almost 124. On a 165Hz monitor that's the difference between occasional dips below your refresh rate and staying consistently above it. On a 240Hz OLED panel, you'll notice the improved frame pacing even more. The 0.1% figure is harder to read too much into since those represent very brief transient spikes, maybe a second or two total over the entire capture window.

### Microsoft Flight Simulator 2024

JFK departure with live air traffic. This game is borderline sadistic with CPU resources. Dense photogrammetry scenery, real-time weather calculations, and hundreds of AI aircraft all compete for the same thread scheduling bandwidth.

| | Avg FPS | 1% Low | 0.1% Low |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| 7800X3D | 68.5 | 44.8 | 31.2 |
| 9800X3D | 76.9 | 53.2 | 38.9 |
| Delta | +12.2% | +18.7% | +24.6% |

A few things to note. The 0.1% lows on the 9800X3D still drop to 38.9 FPS during the heaviest photogrammetry streaming moments — the chip can't prevent every stutter, and a lot of what's happening there is the game's asset pipeline, not raw CPU compute. The 1% low improvement from 44.8 to 53.2 is more representative of the actual experience: approaches and taxiing feel less choppy, and head-tracking in VR (which I didn't test here, but extrapolating from the frametime data) would benefit from fewer deep frame drops.

### Assetto Corsa Competizione — Spa, 30-Car Wet Grid

This is the most CPU-punishing scenario in sim racing that I could set up repeatably. Thirty cars on a wet Spa-Francorchamps means the physics engine is calculating tire grip, spray, collision boxes, and suspension telemetry for every single car on the grid simultaneously. The worst frametimes happen right at the grid start when all 30 cars are bunched into La Source.

| | Avg FPS | 1% Low | 0.1% Low |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| 7800X3D | 148.6 | 98.1 | 68.4 |
| 9800X3D | 164.2 | 114.7 | 84.1 |
| Delta | +10.4% | +16.9% | +22.9% |

### Counter-Strike 2

Dust II deathmatch, 24 players, 1080p competitive settings. I ran this at 1080p instead of 1440p because CS2 players targeting high refresh rates play at 1080p, and I wanted numbers relevant to that use case.

| | Avg FPS | 1% Low | 0.1% Low |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| 7800X3D | 486.2 | 284.1 | 178.6 |
| 9800X3D | 542.8 | 338.4 | 226.5 |
| Delta | +11.6% | +19.1% | +26.8% |

At these framerate levels the absolute numbers don't map neatly to perceived smoothness anymore. Whether you're at 486 or 542 average FPS, your 240Hz or 360Hz monitor is already showing the most recent completed frame. Where it matters is in the 1% lows — during utility spam (multiple smokes, flashbangs detonating simultaneously), the 7800X3D's 1% low of 284 could occasionally dip into the range where a 360Hz display starts dropping frames. The 9800X3D's 338 gives more headroom there, though I want to be careful about overstating this: these are CapFrameX percentile aggregates across three runs, not a guarantee that no individual frame ever took longer.

## A Pattern in the Numbers (and Its Limits)

Looking across all four titles, average FPS gains cluster between 9.6% and 12.2%. The 1% low gains are consistently larger — 16.9% to 19.1%. And the 0.1% lows are larger still.

That pattern makes sense physically. Higher sustained clock speeds and better thermal headroom mean the 9800X3D doesn't need to drop frequencies as aggressively during transient power spikes. When a game engine suddenly needs to schedule a burst of draw calls or physics ticks, the 9800X3D can sustain its boost clock through the spike rather than momentarily throttling. Those momentary throttles are exactly what shows up in low frametime percentiles.

But I want to be honest about the limits of this data. Four games is not a comprehensive suite. I specifically chose titles known to be CPU-sensitive, which means the deltas here probably represent the upper end of what you'd see in practice. A heavily GPU-bound title like Alan Wake 2 at 1440p with ray tracing maxed out would likely show much smaller CPU-driven differences, possibly within margin of error.

## Is It Worth Buying?

This depends entirely on what you already own.

**You have a 7800X3D.** Don't upgrade. Seriously. At 1440p and above, modern games are overwhelmingly GPU-bound. The CPU difference between these two chips only becomes apparent in specific scenarios — crowded open worlds, simulation-heavy engines, competitive 1080p — and even then, the 7800X3D isn't slow. It's still one of the fastest gaming CPUs you can buy. The $449–479 you'd spend on a 9800X3D would do more for your gaming experience going toward a monitor upgrade or a GPU generation jump.

**You're building new on AM5.** The 9800X3D is the one to get, with one caveat. If the 7800X3D drops to the $320–340 range at retail, the price-to-performance ratio shifts. The 7800X3D is still a very strong gaming chip; you're giving up thermal headroom and some frametime consistency, but those are luxury gains, not essential ones. Check pricing the week you're buying.

**You're coming from AM4 or older Intel.** This is where the upgrade case is strongest, and it's not just about FPS numbers. The combination of DDR5 bandwidth, Zen 5 IPC, and 96MB of effective L3 cache changes how modern games feel at a level that's hard to capture in a benchmark table. Asset streaming stutters in Unreal Engine 5 titles — that brief hitch when you turn a corner and the engine loads a new chunk of geometry — largely go away. That's less about clock speed and more about the massive cache absorbing data that would otherwise trigger main memory fetches.
